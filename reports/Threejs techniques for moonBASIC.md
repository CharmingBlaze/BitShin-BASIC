# Borrow three.js shaders, skip its Nanite

The latest three.js is r187, published on 30 October 2026, and its Nanite-like update cannot be ported to moonBASIC as code, though parts of the idea can. `MeshletDrawingPipeline` is an experimental WebGPU-only addon built from compute kernels, storage buffers, atomics and indirect draws, and three.js's own WebGL backend skips it. It also falls short of Nanite: it picks one discrete LOD per object and then culls clusters, with no cluster hierarchy, and I found no streaming. The one open library worth taking is meshoptimizer with its `clusterlod.h` (MIT, offline, no GPU requirements), and a GL 3.3 version would be CPU-selected cluster LOD, a 2005 idea with better tools. moonBASIC's measured scenes do not yet justify building it: they are limited by CPU walks, shadows and fragment work, and two-level discrete LOD with related work already removed about half the cost of its one triangle-bound scene. Most of what else three.js added is more useful, because nearly every screen-space, shadow, material and baked-probe technique is fragment shader work that fits GLSL 330. Worth doing first are hardware-compare Vogel PCF with a real PCSS kernel, wider discrete LOD, Hill ACES and AgX, a GPU-prefiltered HDR sky cube with sky SH, and a `LightProbeGrid`-style baked volume, which is the one new GI method that needs no compute. The main barrier is structural rather than API-level: moonBASIC has only colour and depth, so anything needing stored normals, albedo or velocity (full SSGI, SSR, per-object TAA) costs far more than its shader suggests. The ranking is my judgement: nothing here was run, three.js's numbers come from fast hardware, and moonBASIC has only ever been timed on one Radeon 780M.

## Seven releases moved three.js's new work onto WebGPU

Work between r181 and r187 concentrated on `WebGPURenderer` and TSL, the node shader language that compiles to WGSL or GLSL. The project does not call `WebGPURenderer` the default: its manual says the renderer is "still in an experimental state", and `WebGLRenderer` remains "the recommended choice for pure WebGL 2 applications" with no large features planned ([manual](https://threejs.org/manual/pages/webgpurenderer.html)). The fallback is a real WebGL 2 backend that has no storage buffers and runs `compute()` through transform feedback ([GLSLNodeBuilder](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/r187/src/renderers/webgl-fallback/nodes/GLSLNodeBuilder.js), [WebGLBackend](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/r187/src/renderers/webgl-fallback/WebGLBackend.js)). That matters here because it is a live precedent for running these techniques without compute. Dates below are first npm publication; significance is my reading of release titles, since the notes carry no prose summary.

Three removals carry advice. `DualKawaseBloomNode` was added and removed within r186, with mrdoob saying it "has some artefacts" and cannot match Gaussian bloom ([#33920](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/33920)), which is a caution against choosing a dual filter for moonBASIC's low tiers on benchmark numbers alone. `TiledLighting` went so effort could focus on clustering ([#33751](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/33751)). moonBASIC already matches or passes three.js in several areas, and these are not candidates. Its sky is a physical atmosphere dome, while three.js's `Sky` and `SkyMesh` are still Preetham with 2D clouds ([SkyMesh](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/7e26babbbe69f411112193d04a41baeafb6ebb3b/examples/jsm/objects/SkyMesh.js)). Its water has refraction by scene copy, Beer-Lambert absorption and an FFT option, while three.js's "ocean" examples use flat normal-mapped water ([webgpu_ocean](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/7e26babbbe69f411112193d04a41baeafb6ebb3b/examples/webgpu_ocean.html)). moonBASIC already ships soft GPU particles, FSR 1, real FXAA, progressive bloom, half-res GTAO, CSM in an atlas, tree impostors (three.js has no impostor class) and CPU terrain occlusion (`particles_gpu.go`, `fsr.go`, `fxaa.go`, `postfx.go`, `terrain_impostor.go`, `terrain_occlusion.go`, all under `internal/runtime/`).

**Items 1 to 3 are cheap and aimed at stated goals.** The current PCSS cannot produce the long soft shadows its name promises: `pcssAt` clamps `k` to at most 8 and `pcfAt` turns that into `k * 0.42 * softness`, a radius of about 3.4 atlas texels whatever the blocker distance (`shadow_sample.go:111,143`). I read those lines myself. The filter also compares by hand, since the depth texture has `TEXTURE_COMPARE_MODE` set to none (`shadow_fbo.go:107`), so each of its 9 or 16 taps yields a binary answer. Three.js takes 5 Vogel disk samples rotated by interleaved gradient noise, each a hardware depth comparison ([ShadowFilterNode](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/r187/src/nodes/lighting/ShadowFilterNode.js)). Since hardware comparison filters bilinearly, that should be both cheaper per pixel and smoother (hypothesis). Blocker search still needs raw depth, so bind the atlas twice with [sampler objects](https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/glGenSamplers.xhtml), core in 3.3. Removing the `EnableShadowCache` stub, whose `ShadowMapDyn` is bound and never sampled, frees unit 9 for that in the lit, PBR and terrain programs. The risk is visible noise, since moonBASIC has no temporal filter to average it, although its 16-tap path already uses the same noise. While in the file, adopt three.js's `(p - 0.3) / 0.65` light-bleed remap for the moment filters ([same source](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/r187/src/nodes/lighting/ShadowFilterNode.js)) and either implement `msmAt` properly or stop calling it MSM, since it reads two moments and applies Chebyshev.

**Items 4 and 5 are the real GI story.** `LightProbeGrid` went to `WebGLRenderer` first in r184, with GPU-resident baking and no CPU readback ([#33125](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/33125)). It renders a small cube at each probe (8² per face by default), projects to L2 SH in a fragment pass, and packs seven sub-volumes into one 3D texture ([source](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/r187/examples/jsm/lighting/LightProbeGridWebGL.js)). The single-texture layout is what makes it fit: it takes one sampler, and `mbphysical` has units 6, 7 and 14 free. The lit program `bsshadow` has none by its table, though units 3-6 go unused unless a material carries more than three textures. The costs are elsewhere. A 32×8×32 grid is 8,192 probes and 49,152 cube-face renders, so at an assumed 1 ms per simplified view the bake takes about 49 s (estimate). That argues for a cached bake from proxies, spread over frames as three.js's incremental mode does ([#34486](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/34486)). Its r187 4x bake speedup relies on `ArrayCamera` ([#34909](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/34909)), which has no direct GL 3.3 equivalent. Seven 3D lookups per pixel are also too many for terrain, which already carries a heavy fetch budget and has no free units, so apply the grid to objects, foliage and characters, and let terrain keep its baked horizon occlusion (design; the visual seam between them is untested). A moving sun invalidates the bake, so time-of-day games need incremental rebakes or a sky-only rescale (hypothesis). Build item 4 first, since it shares the SH code and improves every PBR reflection: the cube is CPU-built RGBA8 (`envcube.go:14-25,41-106`), which clips sky radiance. The fragment path takes about 4.6 ms at size 256 against about 1.2 ms for an unmerged compute version, on hardware I could not see ([#35130](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/35130)). Compute is therefore a speedup, not a requirement, and rebuilds can be spread across frames.

**Items 6 to 13 are options above Low, and the budget constrains them.** On the 780M, High runs about 10 ms at 720p, so roughly 1-3 ms is available in heavier scenes (arithmetic on noisy numbers), and a new effect should default off on Low, as `applyLens` already does. Post time is one lump, so split timers for AO, bloom and AA first, in `gpu_passes.go`. New fullscreen passes have no texture-unit pressure, and `applyLens` and the `fillGTAO` pattern are ready plug-in points. Fullscreen passes also cannot yet be exposed to scripts, because user render targets and a custom post chain still do not exist. Building them remains a prerequisite for letting developers do three.js-style effect chains and should stay ahead of items 8 onward in the wider plan.

SMAA has a source conflict to settle by test. Three.js's node says it "should be applied before converting colors to sRGB" ([SMAANode](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/r187/examples/jsm/tsl/display/SMAANode.js)), but the reference implementation says colour edge detection "requires gamma-corrected colors" ([SMAA.hlsl](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/iryoku/smaa/master/SMAA.hlsl)). I would follow the reference and use the existing display-referred FXAA slot. Published cost is 1.02 ms on a GTX 470 at 1080p, from dated hardware ([SMAA paper](https://www.iryoku.com/smaa/downloads/SMAA-Enhanced-Subpixel-Morphological-Antialiasing.pdf)).

**Bitmask AO has a specific reason to beat GTAO here.** The paper's point is that horizon methods "cannot properly simulate light passing behind thin surfaces", while its bit field allows light behind surfaces of constant thickness ([Therrien et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.11376)). Foliage is thin surfaces, so it may reduce the dark canopy halos that horizon AO produces (hypothesis, no published foliage test). Three.js's no-temporal Low preset is 2 slices of 6 steps, 24 samples per pixel ([SSGINode](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/7e26babbbe69f411112193d04a41baeafb6ebb3b/examples/jsm/tsl/display/SSGINode.js)), comparable in scale to moonBASIC's 2-slice, 5-step medium GTAO. The paper claims only "low performance overhead" and I found no timing.

## The missing buffers, not the API, decide what is expensive

The moonBASIC scene target is one RGBA16F colour texture and one depth texture. A search found no second colour attachment anywhere in the runtime, so no normal, albedo, material or velocity buffer exists (`postfx.go:514-531`). Colour alpha is not spare either, since PBR writes `baseColor.a`. Adding an attachment means a second output in `bsshadow`, `mbphysical`, `mbterrain`, `mbwater`, cloud, sky, particle and user programs, plus MSAA renderbuffers. A normal prepass avoids that by redrawing geometry, and redraws are already the engine's largest known cost. This shapes the ranking more than the API limits do.

**Full SSGI illustrates the trap.** The shader itself needs no compute, and it can reconstruct normals from depth. Yet three.js's own example composites `scenePassColor.rgb.mul( ao )` plus `scenePassDiffuse.rgb.mul( gi.rgb )` and fills `diffuseColor` through a scene MRT ([webgpu_postprocessing_ssgi at r187](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/r187/examples/webgpu_postprocessing_ssgi.html)). Without albedo, bounce light cannot be tinted by the surface receiving it. So the honest split is that the AO half (item 10) fits today and the coloured half (item 16) needs the MRT work. Its presets also lean on temporal filtering, and screen-space GI gets little to bounce from in open landscapes where most incoming light is off-screen sky (my reasoning, unmeasured). That is why it ranks below a baked probe volume.

**TAA is a depth-only half measure until velocity exists.** Motion blur already reprojects from depth with a saved view-projection matrix (`postfx_lens.go:96-99`), so a camera-only `TRAANode` port has the data it needs for static scenes, and variance clipping limits the rest. But three.js feeds it a velocity MRT and requires MSAA off ([TRAANode](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/7e26babbbe69f411112193d04a41baeafb6ebb3b/examples/jsm/tsl/display/TRAANode.js)), and Ultra currently enables 4x MSAA. Targets are disposed on size change while dynamic resolution moves in 5% steps, so history needs fixed-size targets rendered through a sub-viewport. Per-object velocity would have to pass previous transforms through G3N, batching proxies, instances and skinning, while merged meshes bake to world space and have no per-object history at all. Wind makes this worse, as swaying trees are what TAA smears most (hypothesis). I would treat camera-only TAA as a High and Ultra trial and judge it on moving foliage.

**SSR stays out, for a firmer reason than before.** The recorded objection (`docs/GRAPHICS.md:218`) leans on a roughly 5 ms figure from a GeForce 650M, which should not be assumed to transfer, and three.js's unmerged rewrite reports about 4.4 ms at 1080p at half resolution on unseen hardware ([#35228](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/35228)). The durable objection is that `SSRNode` needs colour, depth, normal, metalness and roughness ([SSRNode](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/7e26babbbe69f411112193d04a41baeafb6ebb3b/examples/jsm/tsl/display/SSRNode.js)), and water, the main reflective surface, already has planar reflection. `SetWaterSSR` should be renamed or dropped, since it steps inside the reflection texture without a depth test (`water_shaders.go:389-395`).

These techniques are rejected for the base path, each for a stated reason.

## r187's meshlets are a WebGPU addon with per-object LOD, not Nanite

The core change is only a hook: `renderer.drawingPipeline` with a default `StandardDrawingPipeline`, while `MeshletDrawingPipeline` (5,571 lines), `VisibilityBufferDrawingPipeline` (954 lines) and `MeshletGeometryUtils` (417 lines) are addons, and both pipeline classes are labelled experimental ([#35191](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/35191)). Build splits a mesh into six whole-mesh levels at ratios from 1.0 to 0.015, then clusters each level independently at 64 triangles, padded with degenerates ([MeshletGeometryUtils](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/r187/examples/jsm/utils/MeshletGeometryUtils.js)). Compute kernels then cull per object, select the level, cull clusters by frustum, normal cone and two-phase occlusion, and write indirect draws. The visibility pipeline rasterizes triangles up to 8 pixels in software through atomics on 32-bit storage buffers and sends the rest to hardware ([VisibilityBufferDrawingPipeline](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/r187/examples/jsm/pipelines/VisibilityBufferDrawingPipeline.js)). Nanite's defining pieces differ: per-cluster LOD over a DAG, with group boundaries that change per level so seams do not accumulate ([Karis 2021](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)), plus 64-bit visibility and streaming. "No DAG" rests on the build code and the source's phrase "per object". "No streaming" rests on text search only and is the weaker claim. An example description says "per cluster", which the code contradicts, and I trust the code. The PR and class docs also disagree on whether shadow passes use it.

The results make the fit clear. On an RTX 4070 at 1080p, median of two runs, the author reports an unoptimized instanced scene dropping from 230.1 to 3.0 ms and a city from 5.3 to 2.4 ms, while Sponza's GPU time rose from 0.60 to 0.73 ms, and gains shrink in scenes that are fragment-bound or already have LODs and culling ([#35191](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/pull/35191)). The 77x figure relies on GPU-side culling and does not transfer. No independent benchmark or older-GPU result exists, and the feature has been public for about a week.

| Part | Depends on | On GL 3.3 | On an optional GL 4.3 tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| LOD chain, cluster build | meshoptimizer via WASM | Carries over through the C API | Same |
| Cone and sphere tests, self-disabling occlusion policy | TSL compute | Idea only; rewrite in Go | Rewrite in GLSL 430 |
| TSL kernels, depth pyramid in storage buffer | Compute, atomics, indirect dispatch | Nothing carries; Hi-Z by fragment reduction with a late readback | Rewrite by hand, an estimated 1,000-2,000 lines plus every batched vertex stage |
| Instanced 64-triangle chunks with vertex pulling | Storage buffers, indirect draw | Possible with TBO and CPU count, but a new vertex stage in every program | SSBO pulling |
| Scene and node-material plumbing, the largest share | three.js internals | Not applicable | Not applicable |
| Software rasterizer | Compute and atomics | Impossible | Possible, but needs a material resolve pass; reject |
| Hardware visibility buffer | Integer MRT | Technically possible | Adds a deferred material path to a forward engine; reject |

A compute tier is the wrong first step: it would serve only the machines with the most headroom, macOS stops at GL 4.1 ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-us/101525)), and no built-in renderer feature uses `gl4` today. Two design choices are worth copying regardless. Objects the pipeline cannot handle fall back per object to the normal path, and occlusion switches itself off when it culls under 30% ([source](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/r187/examples/jsm/pipelines/MeshletDrawingPipeline.js)).

**Among open libraries, only one is directly reusable.** meshoptimizer and `clusterlod.h` are MIT, have a C API with no GPU requirements, and build a full DAG with hierarchy, and `meshopt_partitionClusters` removes the need for METIS ([clusterlod.h](https://github.com/zeux/meshoptimizer/blob/master/demo/clusterlod.h), [meshoptimizer.h](https://github.com/zeux/meshoptimizer/blob/master/src/meshoptimizer.h)). NVIDIA archived its own builder and points to it ([nv_cluster_lod_builder](https://github.com/nvpro-samples/nv_cluster_lod_builder/blob/main/README.md)). I found no Go binding with these functions, so moonBASIC would write a small cgo wrapper in the way `internal/jolt` is built. MinGW compile status is untested. Every other runtime requires what GL 3.3 lacks: Bevy needs 64-bit atomic textures ([Bevy](https://github.com/bevyengine/bevy/tree/main/crates/bevy_pbr/src/meshlet)), `vk_lod_clusters` needs mesh shaders and compute ([README](https://github.com/nvpro-samples/vk_lod_clusters/blob/main/README.md)), and `three-virtual-geometry` and `nanite-webgpu` need WebGPU compute. These remain references. Nexus has the right old-GPU shape, but its C++ is GPL ([README](https://github.com/cnr-isti-vclab/nexus/blob/master/README.md)). Intel's Masked Occlusion Culling (Apache-2.0, archived) is the one runtime piece needing no GPU feature ([README](https://github.com/GameTechDev/MaskedOcclusionCulling/blob/master/README.md)), and should wait for a measured need.

**The best GL 3.3 design is CPU cluster LOD with large clusters, and it is untested.** Run `clodBuild` on a worker or as a cached bake, at about 256 triangles per cluster, over a single shared vertex buffer. Index data roughly doubles at the 0.5 ratio (geometric series estimate). Restrict it to single static meshes, model children and the merged cells `batch_merge.go` already makes, because those need one transform and can keep existing materials and vertex shaders. Copies stay on instancing with discrete levels, since a multi-draw has no draw identity on core 3.3. Select and cull in Go per view using the header's rule, with shadow views using the texel error `binDepthLODs` already computes. For 1M visible triangles this is about 4,000 clusters and 8,000 tests, about 0.15 ms per view at an assumed 20 ns per test, or about 1 ms across 7 views. Three.js's 64-triangle size would be about four times worse. The larger size also has support: Wihlidal calls 256 the "sweet spot" and blames 64 for command-processor bottlenecks ([GDC 2016](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/optimizing-the-graphics-pipeline-with-compute-gdc-2016/59747720)), and JMS55 reports one multi-draw sub-draw per cluster performing poorly ([Bevy 0.14](https://jms55.github.io/posts/2024-06-09-virtual-geometry-bevy-0-14)). The precedent is Batched Multi-Triangulation, at about 4M triangles and 35 fps on a GeForce 6800 GT ([Cignoni et al.](https://vcg-legacy.isti.cnr.it/Publications/2005/CGGMPS05/BatchedMT_Vis05.pdf)), a result that does not predict moonBASIC's shaded cost.

Submission is the main risk, and no source tests it on the target hardware, so benchmark two routes first. `glMultiDrawElementsBaseVertex` over merged index runs needs no shader change, but it behaves like a loop of draws ([reference](https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL-Refpages/gl4/html/glMultiDrawElementsBaseVertex.xhtml)), and front ends can limit primitive rate at small draw sizes, at 256 triangles per draw as AMD's worst case ([g-truc 2014](https://www.g-truc.net/post-0666.html)). Coarse patches of 2,000-16,000 triangles with plain draws are the proven fallback, at the cost of coarser culling. Streaming one index buffer per frame costs about 12 MB per view per 1M triangles (estimate), and instanced pulling requires a new vertex stage everywhere.

**What it gives up is what makes Nanite Nanite.** There is no software rasterizer, so small triangles still pay quad overdraw, and Nanite's reason for it is that its compute rasterizer is about 3x faster on small clusters ([Karis 2021](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)). Forward shading therefore keeps the error floor at 1 pixel or more, preferably 2 on Low (design). Occlusion stays late or conservative, and there is no per-triangle culling. The system bounds triangle cost but does not make geometry unlimited.

**Does this solve a problem moonBASIC has? Not on current evidence.** The one triangle-bound scene, 4,000 rocks of 8,000 triangles, went from 18.8 to 9.8 ms with discrete LOD and related work (`docs/GRAPHICS.md:82-90`). The city is limited by CPU walks over material state, and cutting its far plane gave no speedup. The island went from 13.6 to about 9.6 ms through cascade turns, cheaper terrain materials and removing the window MSAA, none of them triangle reductions (`docs/GRAPHICS.md:243-249`). Low takes 4.4-6.3 ms at 720p and 9-12 ms at four times the pixels. If scenes keep their order, a line through those gives about 1.5-1.9 ms per 720p of pixels and about 3-4.4 ms that does not scale (estimate), and the Low shadow pass alone is 1.81 ms on the island. So fixed-size work matters, and shadow views are where geometry savings should go first. Assassin's Creed Unity's compute cluster and triangle culling points the same way, with 20-40% of triangles culled in the main view against 30-80% of shadow triangles ([Haar and Aaltonen](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2015/aaltonenhaar_siggraph2015_combined_final_footer_220dpi.pdf)), though a CPU design reaches only the cluster-level part.

There is a real gap, and discrete LOD closes most of it cheaply. A mesh without copies "keeps its full detail" (`batch_lod.go:16`), model children are not grouped, merged cells have no LOD, meshes over 500,000 triangles get none, and the two ratios stop at 0.25 (`batch_lod.go:19-24`) against three.js's 0.015. Note that r187's author, with compute available, still shipped discrete per-object levels and no DAG, which also avoids crack handling. Discrete LOD's limit is that a huge mesh you stand beside stays fully detailed, because distance uses the nearest point of the bounding sphere (`batch_lod.go:230`). Cluster culling covers the off-screen and back-facing parts of that mesh, and only a mesh that must be fine nearby and coarse far away needs the DAG. So the order is: add a 2-5M triangle benchmark and fix `StatsTriangles`, which sums visible geometry rather than drawn triangles (`profiler.go:149-170`), then extend discrete LOD, then add CPU cluster culling, then consider the DAG. Stop as soon as the benchmark passes. Whether scanned or sculpted assets are a target is a product decision that current numbers cannot answer, since scripts today mostly use primitives, copies, generated forests and terrain.

## Conclusion

Three.js's release stream looks like a warning that WebGPU is leaving GL behind, but its own code argues otherwise. Its most useful new GI method shipped on WebGL first, its contact shadows, god rays, TAA and AA nodes are all plain fragment chains, and even its Nanite-like headline chose discrete LOD. What separates moonBASIC from most of these effects is the absence of per-pixel surface data, so the strategic choice is not "when do we need compute" but "will we ever pay for a second attachment in every scene shader". Everything ranked above item 16 avoids answering that, and a good deal of visual gain lies on this side of it. Adding that attachment should be a deliberate decision made once, for full SSGI, velocity and SSR together, and not something each feature drags in separately.

The strongest limit on this ranking is that its costs mostly do not exist yet. No three.js fragment-versus-compute comparison exists on old hardware beyond one PMREM PR, per-effect post timings are missing even on the 780M, and no Intel, NVIDIA or Mac number exists for moonBASIC at all. `moon bench` now exists, so the next useful evidence is cheap: split the post timers, add the high-poly benchmark, and run it on one ten-year-old laptop before building anything past item 3. Several rankings could flip on that result, especially Vogel noise without TAA and whether 3D texture filtering of 16-bit float data, which the probe volume assumes, is dependable on old Intel drivers.
